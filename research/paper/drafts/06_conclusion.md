# 6. Conclusion

For fourteen recent English Pokémon sets valued at dated market prices, under
explicit costs and propagated pull-rate uncertainty, opening boosters is not
a competitive way to acquire cards. In the three main sets, a pack's expected
net resale value is about a quarter to a third of its retail price, and
buying the same cards directly loses less than half as much for the same
budget, with buying decisively better on the mean, the median and the tail.
Chasing one valuable card by opening is rarely cheaper than buying it.

Two mechanisms drive the result. The fixed cost of selling one card exceeds
the value of most cards in a pack, so the value of opening rests on a few
rare hits. And for most sets the expected market value of a pack is below its
retail price even without frictions; for the two sets where it is above
(Paldea Evolved and Temporal Forces), selling costs alone turn the gain into
a loss. Pull-rate uncertainty, which a fixed-rate calculator ignores, widens
every interval but changes no main conclusion.

The results have limits. Prices come from one provider on one date, and card
condition is not specified; European sale prices give the same main
conclusion, but a weekly panel is still being collected. The liquidity
haircut and shipping costs are assumptions, and each card is sold alone,
whereas bundled sales would lower shipping per card for both strategies. The
pull rates come from community openings compiled by a third party, with
reconstructed counts, and a bias common to all openings would not be
detected. Slots are assumed independent and printings equally likely within a
rarity. Finally, the model prices neither the entertainment of opening nor
the cards a collector keeps: at the central estimate, that experience costs
3.07 to 3.46 USD per pack. The framework needs only per-rarity pull rates
with sample sizes, a documented slot layout, a typed price snapshot and
explicit costs, and one configuration file reruns it on a new set, date or
cost assumption.
